Consensus Facts

  • An anonymous federal official, represented by the nonprofit Whistleblower Aid, filed a disclosure alleging serious problems with the U.S. Postal Service's new mail ballot verification system.
  • The disclosure was submitted to Democratic Sen. Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, who sent it to Postmaster General David Steiner in a letter dated Monday and made it public this week.
  • The system stems from an executive order President Trump signed in March directing the federal government to generate lists of eligible U.S. citizens and requiring USPS to transmit ballots only to voters on state-submitted lists.
  • USPS finalized a rule last month laying out the new mail-voting requirements, which was published in the Federal Register.
  • Under the system, state election officials must submit voter names, addresses, and unique ballot envelope barcodes to a new online federal portal; USPS scans ballot envelopes and checks them against this data.
  • If even a single ballot barcode in a batch (cited as roughly one bad ballot in a batch of 10,000) fails to scan or match, the entire batch is rejected and returned to state officials.
  • The whistleblower alleges the system was rushed—built in weeks or a few months rather than the nine to twelve months normally needed for a project of this complexity.
  • A federal judge issued a temporary block/restraining order against USPS's plan last week, and the Justice Department/administration has appealed that decision.
  • The whistleblower's lawyers characterized the process as 'entirely unforgiving,' warning it could delay thousands of ballots in repeated verification cycles and prevent states from mailing enormous numbers of ballots.
  • 23 Democratic-led states plus Washington, D.C. have sued the administration to challenge Trump's executive order.
  • USPS and the White House did not respond to requests for comment by the time of publication.
  • Trump has claimed mail-in voting fraud justifies the changes, has himself voted by mail, and his administration has not put forward evidence of widespread fraud (studies and audits show noncitizen voting is extremely rare).

Disagreements

When USPS began building the portal / timeline specifics
NBC News: Whistleblower says USPS didn't begin building the portal 'in earnest' until June, giving less than three months; normally would take nine to twelve months.
Axios: Says the system was built in 'weeks, not the nine months to a year normally needed.'
CNN: Emphasizes IT teams were to be given less than a week to test the finished system, with only four days for user testing.
Status of the court order regarding continued development
NBC News: A federal court order Monday stated the government could continue building its portal as long as states aren't required to participate.
NPR: Key parts of the plan, including state compliance requirements, remain blocked; a Boston judge issued a temporary restraining order last week.
CNN: A judge last week froze the mandate but said USPS could still offer the portal to states that voluntarily upload voter info.
Axios: U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani issued blocks, but the Supreme Court later upheld the order, saying states sued too early; states have filed a new lawsuit.
Whether USPS defied court orders while building the system
Axios: Frames it as a central allegation—whistleblower says the agency defied court orders while rushing to build the system.
NBC News: Whistleblower said USPS ordered workers to continue developing despite court orders, but notes a Monday order permitted continued building without state participation.
CBS News: States the whistleblower accuses the agency of 'flouting court rulings' by continuing implementation.
Scanning method — full batch vs. sampling
CBS News: Says mail workers scan a sample of a batch—about 400 out of 10,000 or more.
CNN: Says either the entire batch or a sampling is scanned, depending on where ballots are dropped.
Whether the Supreme Court has ruled on the order
Axios: States the Supreme Court upheld the order on procedural grounds (states sued too early), without weighing the merits.
NPR: Says the Supreme Court has not yet weighed in on the legality of Trump's directives.
